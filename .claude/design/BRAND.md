# Brand & Visual Identity

## Audience

Public high school students in Texas, grades 9–12, in career and workforce
readiness courses. Mixed reading levels. Mixed engagement levels. Some have
ADHD, learning disabilities, or English-language-learner status. The
materials need to **work for the student in the third row who is half
checked-out** — not just the eager-front-row student.

The secondary audience is **other teachers** who may eventually use these
materials. Materials should look professional enough that a colleague would
print and use them without modification.

## Voice

- **Direct, never preachy.** "Acknowledge the customer first" — not "It is
  important to acknowledge the customer first."
- **Named, never numbered.** "Taking a Pause" — not "Strategy #1." Numbered
  strategies don't transfer; named ones stick.
- **Plain English.** If a vocab word is essential, define it on the sheet.
  If it's optional, cut it.
- **One step at a time.** Multi-part questions get broken into separate
  prompts. Hint text sits next to the prompt it hints, not above the section.

## Visual identity

The worksheets are **printable-first**. Every design choice is filtered
through "will this look good on a black-and-white printer at a high school?"
That means:

- **High-contrast text on white background.** Color is for emphasis, not
  decoration. A worksheet should still be fully usable in grayscale.
- **Two pages, front-and-back, every time.** This is a hard constraint.
  If content can't fit, prune content — never let layout suffer.
- **Theme color used sparingly** — title, section headers, framework labels,
  one or two callout boxes. Body text stays black.
- **Generous whitespace** between sections. The page should feel walkable,
  not crammed.
- **Fill-in lines, not boxes.** Where students write, use a horizontal
  underline. Boxes look like cells; lines look like writing space.

## Theme system

A worksheet picks one **theme color** (blue, purple, teal, brown, green,
orange, navy) and uses it consistently for: title, section headers,
framework label colors. The light variant of the same theme is used for
info-box backgrounds.

Theme choice is loosely tied to topic:
- **Blue** — interview, communication, general professional
- **Teal** — customer service, calm-first topics, attention/perception
- **Purple** — conflict resolution, emotional intelligence
- **Green** — goal-setting, growth, success topics
- **Orange** — resume, application, action-oriented
- **Brown** — practical/manual topics
- **Navy** — soft skills chapters (Soft Skills textbook), formal topics

These are conventions, not rules. A teacher knows the room better than the
template does.

## What we don't do

- **No emoji in body text.** Emoji is reserved for callout-box icons (💡
  tip, ⚠️ warning, 🔗 connection, ☐ checkbox).
- **No stock photography.** Worksheets are typographic.
- **No drop shadows or gradients.** Flat color, simple borders.
- **No lengthy preambles.** A worksheet starts with the activity, not with
  paragraphs of context.
- **No corporate-feel branding.** The worksheets aren't trying to look like
  a SaaS dashboard. They look like good teacher materials.

## When designing new artifacts

Ask: "would this fit on the table next to a printed worksheet without
looking like it came from a different planet?" If yes, the design is on
brand. If it feels glossier, more decorated, or more colorful than that —
it's drifted.
